Grapevine

From a sales call and a spreadsheet to one platform for leaders, members, and nonprofits

When I came on, Grapevine was the first product aimed at giving circles, a fast-growing form of philanthropy where fifty people put in $10 each, decide together where it goes, and give $500. The product hadn't caught up: a homepage, a few forms, and a sales team walking every leader through setup by hand, while the circles themselves still ran on email, donation collection by check or Venmo, and someone's spreadsheet.

Over a five year engagement I led Product Design across the platform, working directly with the CEO and CTO. I designed the experiences that replaced them: leaders launch and run circles on their own, members join understanding what they're joining, and the money reaches the nonprofit through the same place the decision was made.

I led the full design of Grapevine's platform, including a new homepage that communicated the value of community-based giving and the impact circles had created.

The leader problem

A circle leader is a volunteer, not an operator, so the tools were whatever was at hand. A spreadsheet for members, a Word doc for the donation history, an email list for announcements, and a stack of paper checks for the giving itself. Keeping a group together was more overhead than enjoyment.

Moving a circle onto the platform meant a call with a rep walking the leader through setup, which got the first circles on and was never going to scale past them.

Outcomes

  • Self-service setup replaced hands-on sales onboarding, so a leader launches a circle without a call
  • Spreadsheets, Word docs, and email lists consolidated into one dashboard
  • Donations collected and distributed through the platform, eliminating paper checks and manual tracking
  • CSV import brought a circle's existing history and roster in on day one

Getting leaders off the ground

Onboarding was the whole problem. A leader who stalls at setup never launches, and every leader who stalled was a salesperson's afternoon.

I designed a mobile-first progressive onboarding flow that broke setup into three stages, each asking a little more as the leader's confidence built. First, they define the circle's purpose and donation goal. Second, they finalize the group page and invite collaborators. Third, they make the first donation and invite members. By the time a leader launched, they understood the product well enough to bring others into it.

The first stage is intentionally light. A few questions frame the giving circle before a leader moves on to customizing the group page.

The second stage matters more than it looks. Rather than a settings form, I put leaders directly into their own group homepage, editing in place. Seeing the circle take shape, with its name and its cause and its goal, is what makes a leader want to launch it. Remaining setup steps stay pinned at the top of the page with a prominent Launch Group CTA to signal the finish line.

The second stage opens the giving circle page up for customization. Editing in place lets a leader see and feel the group before launching it, with setup steps and the launch CTA held at the top.

Launching was designed as a moment worth celebrating: confetti, an encouraging message, and a clear next step. The first donation is held until this point deliberately. A leader who gives before launching has an inactive circle they have already paid into, and the goal was to send them into member recruitment feeling confident, not uncertain.

Launching triggers a celebration moment with one clear next step: be the first to donate. Holding the donation until launch meant leaders never had an inactive circle they had already given to.

Tools built for how leaders actually work

The first version of the leader experience was the member view with edit and open CTAs layered on top, a WYSIWYG approach that made sense early on but couldn't scale as more leaders joined the platform. As usage grew, it became clear that leaders needed real management tools, not just the ability to edit their group page.

The leader dashboard brought together everything needed to run a giving circle, discussion posts, donation cycle management, a lightweight member CRM, and group settings in one place.

Most circles arriving on Grapevine already had a history. They had given for years and tracked it in a spreadsheet. I designed CSV import for exactly that, letting a leader bring in their existing roster and past giving so the platform started with their record instead of asking them to abandon it. Meeting a circle where it already was, which was a spreadsheet, was the difference between a leader trying Grapevine and a leader moving to it.

Circles arrive with years of history in a spreadsheet. Import brings the roster and past giving in on day one, so a leader starts with their record rather than abandoning it.

Moving money without paper checks

The giving itself was the last thing still happening off-platform. A circle voted, and then the leader wrote a check, mailed it, and typed the amount into a doc so the group had a record.

I designed collection and distribution into the platform. Members give per cycle, the funds pool against the cycle, and once the group votes, the leader submits the donation to the winning nonprofit directly. The record keeps itself. The change was less about the payment than about what stopped being a leader's job: no chasing individual members for their share, no check to mail, no ledger to maintain by hand, and a running total the whole group can see.

Once the vote closes, the leader sends the cycle's donations to the winning nonprofit from the same place the group decided. No check to mail, no ledger to update.

What changed for leaders

A volunteer can move their circle onto Grapevine on a Tuesday night and launch it before bed, without talking to anyone. Running it afterward is one dashboard instead of four documents, and the money moves through the same place the decision was made. The sales-led motion that got the first circles on stopped being the only way a circle could get there.
